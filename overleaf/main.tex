\begin{abstract}
This report documents the integration of Carolus/uVGS-2 -- a monocular,
4-LED-beacon pose estimation system derived from NASA's SVGS and originally
developed for the Astrobee free-flyer -- onto a rooted RoboMaster S1 ground
robot, with the longer-term goal of relocalization by fusing Carolus with
the MINS (Multi-sensor-aided Inertial Navigation System) framework in place
of MINS's GPS channel.

The principal result is a derived and hardware-validated conversion from
Carolus's pose convention to the ROS frame. Two prior integrations of
Carolus onto other platforms had found their conversion by trial and error,
and for this robot it had never been found at all. We derive it from the
solver's own cost function rather than from documentation, correcting two
independent errors in the inherited implementation --- a wrong determinant
claim and, underneath it, a wrong direction --- and validate it on hardware
by rotating the beacon about each camera axis in turn: \textbf{zero axes
inverted}, with each expected sign stated before its measurement. The
derived operator agrees to $3.6\times10^{-16}$ with the constant a parallel
integration had arrived at empirically, which explains that result rather
than merely matching it.

We also report a working autonomous approach pipeline validated end to end
on hardware; a lightweight EKF fusing wheel odometry with Carolus absolute
pose, validated as a wiring sanity check rather than an accuracy claim; a
5.95$\times$ throughput improvement from relocating the vision node; and a
diagnosis of the platform's attitude estimate as an uncorrected
gyro-integration artefact, closed against a calibrated optical reference.
Full MINS integration and a Carolus-versus-AprilTag orientation comparison
remain open, staged deliberately behind the lower-risk items.

A recurring methodological theme is documented alongside the technical
results, because it shaped them: a substantial fraction of the time lost on
this project went to instruments that reported success by being structurally
incapable of reporting failure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Setup}

\subsection{Hardware}
The platform is a RoboMaster S1, rooted via \texttt{s1\_sdk\_hack\_v0.0.5},
which exposes the RoboMaster EP SDK (chassis drive, gimbal control, onboard
camera, wheel odometry, and IMU-derived attitude) over a local API. A
Raspberry Pi 4B is mounted at the turret position and connects to the robot
over USB/RNDIS at a fixed address. The Pi runs the vision and control nodes;
a separate lab PC runs the operator GUI and the localization back end.

\subsection{Carolus / uVGS-2}
Carolus is a 4-LED beacon detection and P4P (Perspective-4-Point) pose
solver, implemented with Ceres, inherited from prior work at FIT and derived
from the original NASA SVGS system used on Astrobee. It publishes a 6-DOF
pose (camera frame) whenever the beacon is visible. It runs on the Pi, at
roughly 13\,Hz with a beacon in view. It originally ran on the lab PC at
2.5\,Hz, consuming frames streamed from the robot; that gap was image
transport rather than computation, and relocating the node closed it (see
Results).

\subsection{Software Stack}
ROS Noetic, run on Ubuntu 22.04 on the lab PC (not the officially supported
target for either ROS Noetic or several dependencies used here, which
required local build workarounds -- see the repository's \texttt{cmake\_shims/}).
A Tkinter GUI (\texttt{shortcuts/carolus\_launcher.py}) provides manual
teleoperation, a live telemetry dashboard, and sequential launch of the ROS
stack --- see Section~\ref{sec:launcher}.

\section{Project History and Continuity with Prior Work}
\label{sec:history}

Per the project supervisor's explicit guidance,\footnote{Email correspondence,
2026-07-03: ``For the documentation it seems the best would be to use
Overleaf. We can start with the documents left by Quentin, eliminate the
portions that are wrong, and document the new solutions you are

project's prior iteration, keeps what was correctly worked out, and states
explicitly which parts were found not to work and why -- rather than
silently discarding that history.

\subsection{Prior Architecture (Quentin, March 2026)}
The hardware identification (rooted S1, Pi 4B at the turret, RNDIS link),
the Carolus/UVGS-2 lineage from NASA's Astrobee SVGS system, and the general
shape of the pipeline (beacon detection $\to$ pose $\to$ motion command)
were correctly established by the previous iteration of the project and
carried forward unchanged.\footnote{Supervisor's own assessment, email
2026-07-03: ``the hardware identification, the Astrobee/UVGS-2 lineage, and
the general pipeline shape were all correctly worked out and saved me real
time.''} The control layer itself, however, was substantially different
from the current one and was not carried forward. Quentin's tutorial
(\emph{Carolus Robot -- Full Operation Tutorial}, March 2026) documents a
five-pane \texttt{tmux} launcher (\texttt{run\_stack.sh}) starting, in
order, \texttt{roscore} with the Pi's \texttt{eth1} interface brought up
manually, a camera-capture script, \texttt{rm\_cam\_ros.py}, a
\texttt{rosbridge\_websocket} server, and \texttt{testcarolus.launch}
(Source: Carolus Tutorial.pdf, \S3, p.2--3). The control script itself
(\texttt{testco6.py}) ran in a separate Python 3.7 virtual environment and
communicated with ROS over \texttt{roslibpy}/rosbridge rather than a direct
\texttt{rospy} import -- a workaround for a Python-version mismatch between
that environment and the SDK (Source: Carolus Tutorial.pdf, \S4--5,
p.3--6). It implemented a single reactive control law, not a state machine:
a PID controller on the beacon bearing (\texttt{bearing = atan2(x, -z)}
computed from \texttt{/pose}) drove the chassis to rotate in place until
aligned within a deadband, then to advance at a fixed forward speed until
within a target stopping distance, with the gimbal centered
(\texttt{gimbal.set\_mode(0)}, i.e.\ free-coupling mode, then
\texttt{moveto(pitch=0, yaw=0)}) and left fixed for the whole
maneuver (Source: Carolus Tutorial.pdf, \S4 and \S7, p.3--8). The tutorial
also documents an IMU recalibration procedure via the RoboMaster mobile app,
to be run if motion appears unstable (Source: Carolus Tutorial.pdf, \S6,
p.7).

\subsection{Errors Identified in the Prior Iteration}
\label{sec:prior-bugs}
The supervisor's own review of the roughly fifteen control-script
iterations left in the repository (beyond the specific version documented
in the tutorial above) identified a chain of compounding bugs, communicated
directly and quoted here rather than paraphrased, since the exact wording
carries the diagnosis:\footnote{All four items and the quoted text in this
subsection: email correspondence with the project supervisor, 2026-07-03.}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A duplicated topic advertisement in the C++ pose node.}
  ``The C++ pose estimation node had a topic that got declared twice in the
  code, so the second declaration silently overwrote the first and one of
  the two topics it was supposed to publish on never actually went out. Most
  of the control script iterations subscribed to exactly that dead topic.''
  This traces to \texttt{carolus\_astrobee.cpp}, where the node
  advertises a parameterized \texttt{pose\_topic\_} (intended as
  \texttt{ff\_msgs::VisualLandmarks}) immediately before unconditionally
  advertising \texttt{/pose} (\texttt{geometry\_msgs::PoseStamped}); if the
  two names coincide, only one type survives and the other silently never
  publishes. The current codebase avoids the collision by keeping
  \texttt{pose\_topic} set to its own distinct (if otherwise unused) name
  and treating \texttt{/pose} as the single source of truth, rather than by
  fixing the underlying double-advertisement in the inherited C++ file.
  \item \textbf{A constructor typo in the specific script matching the
  tutorial.} ``The specific script matching Quentin's tutorial had a typo
  in its constructor (it was declared as \texttt{init} instead of
  \texttt{\_\_init\_\_}), so it never actually initialized itself when
  run.'' This was identified by the supervisor in the deployed script file
  itself, not independently re-verified in the present report against the
  copy of the code printed in the tutorial PDF (which shows a correctly
  double-underscored constructor); the two need not be the same revision of
  the file.
  \item \textbf{An earlier EKF attempt that diverged and was never
  resolved.} ``There was also an attempt earlier in that period to fuse IMU
  and wheel odometry with an EKF node, which is a reasonable thing to try,
  but the logs show it diverged (NaN and denormalized quaternion errors)
  and was never resolved.'' This is a distinct issue from the current
  project's own \texttt{robot\_localization} EKF (Section~\ref{sec:f3}),
  which is a fresh implementation rather than a continuation of this
  earlier, abandoned one; the earlier attempt's divergence is recorded here
  as motivation for validating the current EKF carefully (an unrelated
  frame-assumption bug was in fact later found and fixed in the current
  implementation, Section~\ref{sec:lessons}) rather than assuming a
  from-scratch EKF is automatically safe from this class of failure.
  \item \textbf{Simultaneous SDK connections silently blocking motion.}
  ``Two of the ROS nodes were opening simultaneous connections to the
  robot's SDK, which I later confirmed silently blocks movement commands
  after a few calls, something that would have made any of that control
  code unreliable even if the topic and constructor issues had been
  fixed.'' The supervisor further notes that this was already suspected in
  the prior iteration but not resolved there: ``Quentin found this problem
  but he was not able to fix it: used rosbridge to transfer Carolus data,
  but then he was unable to send motion commands'' -- which explains the
  rosbridge/Python-3.7-venv split architecture described above as an
  unsuccessful workaround attempt, not an arbitrary design choice. The
  current architecture avoids the failure mode directly, by design, with a
  single shared SDK connection rather than a fix layered on top of multiple
  connections (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).
\end{enumerate}

None of the four issues above are present in the current implementation:
the first is sidestepped by topic-naming discipline, the second and third
do not apply to it (built from scratch, not a continuation of either
buggy file), and the fourth is avoided architecturally rather than patched.
This distinction -- avoided by a different design versus actively
fixed -- is kept explicit throughout this report rather than collapsed into
a single ``resolved'' status, per the supervisor's own request to document
changes to both the code and the reasoning behind them.

\section{System Architecture}

\subsection{Autonomous State Machine}
The core autonomy behavior is a state machine with four states:
\textbf{SEARCH} (gimbal sweeps to look for the beacon without moving the
chassis; a 6-heading ``fan search'' pattern rotates the chassis through
successive headings if the beacon is not found from the starting
orientation), \textbf{ALIGN} (chassis rotates in place to face the beacon
once detected), \textbf{APPROACH} (visual servoing: the gimbal tracks the
beacon while the chassis closes distance along the same heading), and
\textbf{STOP} (triggered within a target stopping distance). SEARCH through
APPROACH through STOP was validated end to end on hardware; LOCATE (a mode
for calibrating beacon position live on an operator-drawn 2D map) and the
fan-search coverage pattern were added and validated afterward. A structural
bug in ALIGN (a pose buffer cleared immediately before the state needed to
read it, preventing the state from ever completing) was identified and
fixed.

\subsection{Safety and Operator Tooling}
All hardware sessions are driven from a single operator GUI
(\texttt{carolus\_launcher.py}), described in Section~\ref{sec:launcher}.
Beyond launching the stack it provides manual piloting, a live telemetry
dashboard, and the safety interlocks the autonomous modes depend on --- a
deadman timeout on both chassis and gimbal, and a forward-motion block on
the time-of-flight sensor.

\section{The Carolus-to-ROS Orientation Conversion}
\label{sec:quaternion}

This section is the project's principal result. The supervisor's framing was
that both prior integrations of Carolus onto other platforms had found their
coordinate conversion \emph{by trial and error}, and that for the RoboMaster
``the quaternion solution was never found'' --- so what was wanted was not a
working constant but a derivation, with the method shown.

\subsection{Two artefacts that could not both be right}

The starting point was a contradiction between two documents this project
had inherited and was about to reproduce. The technical manual stated that
Carolus's native frame is left-handed and that conversion to the ROS
convention (REP-103) is therefore \emph{improper}, with determinant $-1$.
The TF broadcaster actually running on the robot used a matrix whose
determinant is $+1$ --- a proper rotation. One of the two was false and had
already been published.

Resolving it turned out to matter more than expected, because underneath the
determinant question sat a second and independent error in the conversion's
\emph{direction}.

\subsection{Derivation from the solver's own cost function}

Rather than infer the convention from documentation, it was read out of the
code that computes the pose. Carolus's P4P residual
(\texttt{ceresP4P.hpp}) evaluates
\[
p_{\text{camera}} = \mathcal{R}(\texttt{camera\_R})\, p_{\text{beacon}} + t,
\]
the standard PnP extrinsic convention, so the solver's rotation is
$R_{\text{beacon}\rightarrow\text{camera}}$. The node then transposes it
before publishing, so the pose on \texttt{/pose} carries
$R_{\text{camera}\rightarrow\text{beacon}}$. The TF edge
\texttt{camera\_link} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{beacon\_observed} requires the
opposite direction, by TF2's parent-child convention --- the inverse of what
is published. The inherited formula never inverted anything.

Combined with the determinant question, the conversion is a similarity
transform, not a component permutation:
\[
\boxed{\;q_{\text{ROS}} = r \otimes q_{\text{cam}}^{-1} \otimes r^{-1}\;}
\]
with the position remap $(x,y,z)_{\text{ROS}} = (-z,\, x,\, -y)_{\text{cam}}$.

Two supporting results are worth recording. First, a dispatched research
round returned four independent derivations that all reached $\det = +1$
from REP-103's own declaration that both frames are right-handed --- a
proper change of basis between two same-handedness frames is $\det=+1$ by
definition, not by convergent opinion. The manual's claim was retracted with
a visible correction rather than a silent edit. Second, the new formula was
checked against the constant that the Limo integration had found empirically
by trial and error: over eight random unit quaternions the two agree to
$3.6\times10^{-16}$, while the old formula differs by $0.97$ to $1.28$ on
every trial. That the two independent routes --- our derivation and their
guessing --- land on the same operator is the strongest desk-level evidence
available, and it explains their result rather than merely matching it.

\subsection{Two properties that constrain how it can be tested}

Both emerged from the same research round and both changed the experimental
protocol before it was run.

Conjugation is \emph{blind to the sign} of the change-of-basis matrix:
$(-B)R(-B)^{-1} \equiv BRB^{-1}$, since $\det(-B) = -\det(B)$ in odd
dimensions. A hardware test comparing our matrix against its own negation
would therefore learn nothing, however carefully executed. Any discriminating
test must use a genuinely different improper candidate --- a single-axis
flip.

And $180^\circ$ test rotations are \emph{degenerate}: proper and improper
conjugation coincide exactly there. The protocol's own draft had specified
$90^\circ$ rotations on all three axes and had to be corrected before use:
a full $90^\circ$ tilt or pan turns the flat four-LED array edge-on to the
camera and loses detection at precisely the orientation being measured. The
tested angles were reduced to roughly $30^\circ$, comfortably clear of both
$0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.

\subsection{What made the measurement possible}

The hardware validation was attempted across several sessions before it
succeeded, and none of the obstacles were in the mathematics.

The first was ours. The TF broadcaster republished the transform only when
the pose had changed by more than $10^{-3}$ --- a bandwidth optimisation
that is wrong for TF specifically, because \texttt{tf2} expires buffer
entries after about ten seconds. A transform that stops being republished
stops \emph{existing}: \texttt{tf\_echo} reports ``frame does not exist''
rather than ``stale''. With the robot and the beacon both stationary ---
exactly the rig this measurement requires, and exactly the case where the
pose legitimately does not change --- the frame silently vanished. The more
stable the setup, the less the measurement existed.

The second was the rig. Every hand-held attempt translated the beacon while
rotating it, so the reading mixed a rotation with a displacement and could
not be read for sign. Mounting the beacon on a rigid tripod removed that
degree of freedom, and the improvement is visible in the data: the fraction
of samples landing on the P4P solver's alternate near-planar solution fell
from 3.6--12\% to \textbf{0.0\%}, and the interquartile spread of the
readings from 1--2.7$^\circ$ to 0.18--0.32$^\circ$.

The third was detection. Beacon over-brightness does not degrade gracefully:
past a threshold the four LEDs bloom into a single merged blob that fails
both the area and the circularity filters, and the detector then reports
\texttt{Not enough blobs with required circularity} followed by
\texttt{0 contours found} --- wording that reads as \emph{beacon absent}
when the truth is the exact opposite. Measured directly, the frame carried
\textbf{88\,628 saturated pixels, 9.6\% of its area}. Since the beacon's
intensity control is a physical knob with no readout, a live meter was
written to make the setting visible while it is turned.

\subsection{Result}

Each axis was rotated by approximately $30^\circ$, held still, and measured
by averaging many de-duplicated samples. \textbf{The expected sign was
written down before each measurement}, which is what makes the outcome a
result rather than an interpretation. Each rotation was bracketed between
two reference readings so that any residual drift could be measured from
their difference and subtracted, rather than assumed negligible.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\hline
Axis & Gesture & Measured \\
\hline
Yaw (Z) & tripod pan, $\sim30^\circ$ clockwise from above & $-28.8^\circ$ \\
Pitch (Y) & tripod tilt, $\sim30^\circ$ backward & $-23.7^\circ$ \\
Roll (X) & hand twist, $\sim30^\circ$, steering-wheel motion & $-23.6^\circ$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Zero axes inverted.} Each rotation landed on its own axis, the other
two moving by at most a few degrees --- the residual of an imperfect hand
gesture rather than cross-talk. Every measured sign matches the derivation.
The drift correction moved each answer by less than half a degree, so the
bracketing turned out to be unnecessary; it was run anyway, because that is
what proves the statement rather than assuming it.

One methodological trap is worth recording, because it nearly produced a
fourth measurement of an axis already closed. A rigid tripod's own rotation
is a vertical pan --- which is yaw, not roll. Instructions phrased as
``spin the beacon in its own plane'' sent the operator back to that same
mechanism twice, and the reading reproduced the yaw result almost exactly
under the roll label. It was caught by comparing the two readings side by
side: two physically different gestures should not produce the same
signature, and these were not different gestures. Genuine roll requires
rotation about the camera-to-beacon \emph{line}, which a standard tripod
head does not offer at all, and which had to be done by hand.

\section{Localization and Sensor Fusion Methodology}

\subsection{F3: Lightweight EKF (\texttt{robot\_localization})}
\label{sec:f3}
Wheel odometry (from the SDK) and Carolus absolute pose (computed from a
known, hand-measured beacon position and the detected camera-to-beacon
transform) are fused with \texttt{robot\_localization}, a loosely-coupled,
widely used ROS EKF. This was chosen as the first step ahead of MINS because
it is lighter weight, has no unusual hardware/OS support requirements beyond
what was already being worked around, and gives an early, concrete
localization result to build confidence in the sensor and transform chain
before committing effort to a heavier framework.

\subsection{F4: MINS --- built and characterised, integration pending}
The longer-term goal is to replace MINS's GPS channel with Carolus, since
Carolus provides exactly the kind of periodic, absolute-pose correction that
GPS provides outdoors, applied here to indoor navigation. MINS is officially
supported on Ubuntu 20.04/ROS Noetic (not the 22.04 environment used on the
lab PC), builds entirely in user space (no superuser access is available in
this environment), and expects its GPS-equivalent channel as a standard
\texttt{sensor\_msgs/NavSatFix} message, which will require a small adapter
node from Carolus's native \texttt{PoseWithCovarianceStamped} output. The
plan is to first validate the MINS installation itself against its official
KAIST Urban benchmark dataset (with independent ground truth) before
attempting any integration with RoboMaster hardware or Carolus data,
so that installation problems and integration problems are never diagnosed
as the same problem. The intended comparison metric between F3 and F4 is
RMSE against ground truth plus NEES/NIS consistency metrics, for which MINS
already ships an evaluation toolbox.

\subsection{F5: Carolus vs. AprilTag Orientation Accuracy (planned)}
A secondary, more exploratory objective is to quantify whether Carolus's
non-coplanar 4-LED beacon geometry gives a more accurate orientation (roll/
pitch/yaw) estimate than a planar fiducial marker such as AprilTag, which is
a plausible but not yet validated hypothesis (published AprilTag accuracy
figures are already on the order of 1--2 degrees). This is treated
explicitly as a hypothesis to test, not an assumed result, and is one of the
project's potential original contributions if confirmed. There is a
supporting theoretical argument: in pure visual-inertial navigation, yaw
about the gravity vector is a known unobservable direction and a documented
source of filter inconsistency; an absolute-orientation beacon reference
such as Carolus directly resolves that weakness.

\subsection{Ground-Truth Drift Measurement Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
To quantify the localization-drift reduction that Carolus provides -- the
core quantitative deliverable requested by the project supervisor -- a
dedicated protocol was defined: a 10--15\,m course with 4--5 intermediate
control points (not just start/end) marked on the floor and measured against
a CAD drawing of the corridor with a measuring tape, a minimum of 3 runs per
mode (wheel odometry alone vs.\ EKF with Carolus), for 6 runs total. For each
control point and run, position error against the surveyed ground truth is
computed; the reporting metrics are an error-vs-distance curve, the
normalized final error (as a percentage of distance travelled), and RMSE per
mode. Expected drift magnitude for uncalibrated wheel odometry over this
distance is on the order of 1--5\% (10--75\,cm), well above the
centimeter-level noise floor, so the effect should be clearly visible if
present.

\section{Operational Tooling: the Carolus Launcher}
\label{sec:launcher}

Every hardware session in this project runs through a single Tkinter
application, \texttt{carolus\_launcher.py}. It is worth a section of its own
not because a GUI is a research contribution, but because its feature list
is a fairly precise record of what the investigation actually needed ---
most of its panels exist because some specific failure was invisible without
them.

\subsection{What it does}

It starts the whole stack over SSH in dependency order, each stage unlocking
only once the previous one is genuinely up --- \texttt{roscore} checked by
port, the camera by the topic actually publishing, rather than by the
subprocess having been spawned. It drives the robot manually from the
keyboard. And it shows a live dashboard: battery, attitude, wheel speeds,
velocity, a camera preview, Carolus's own blob-detection view, and beacon
detection state.

\subsection{Why it looks the way it does}

Three constraints shaped the architecture, and all three came from bugs.

The GUI never opens an SDK connection. The RoboMaster EP accepts exactly one
control connection, and a second one does not fail cleanly --- it partially
establishes, and calls then hang indefinitely with no exception raised. That
was diagnosed early, from a symptom (``the robot stops responding after two
or three commands'') that gave no indication of its cause. Everything the
GUI sends to the robot therefore travels as text over a pipe to a small
relay process, which republishes it on ROS topics that the single connected
node subscribes to.

The dashboard reads telemetry out of the node's own log stream rather than
querying the robot, for the same reason.

And every command path carries a deadman. A held key emits no repeated X11
events, so an early version sent one command and let the chassis stop
half a second later with the key still down; the gimbal, meanwhile, had no
timeout at all --- its last commanded speed was re-sent at 20\,Hz
indefinitely, so a lost key-release event left it turning until something
else intervened.

\subsection{The panels that exist because of a specific failure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Blob-detection view.} Carolus already published its own
  thresholded image with each selected blob circled; nothing needed adding
  to the detector, only a panel to show it. It answers ``is the detector
  seeing four LEDs or four reflections'' directly, which no log line does.

  \item \textbf{Beacon signature (saturated pixel count and hue).} Added
  after over-brightness was found to present in the log as beacon
  \emph{absence} (Section~\ref{sec:quaternion}). The intensity control is a
  physical knob with no readout; without this panel the operator is tuning
  blind. Judged against a recorded known-good signature, so a knocked
  beacon can be detected rather than discovered.

  \item \textbf{Session logs on disk.} The log panes were memory-only and
  truncated at 300 lines. A planned ``free'' check --- grep an earlier run
  for a specific tag --- turned out to have no data behind it: the runs had
  happened and the evidence had been discarded. Every line is now appended
  to a per-session file, tagged by which terminal produced it. A
  quantitative cross-check performed ten days later drew entirely on data
  captured, unrequested, by that change.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{An honest note on its verification}

Most of the launcher's own defects were found by running it, not by
reviewing it, and one case is worth stating because it recurs throughout
this project: a refactor bounded by a regular expression silently removed
157 lines of module-level code, including a function that other code
depended on. \texttt{py\_compile} passed cleanly, because every removed name
is only looked up at runtime. The failure appeared instantly the first time
the window was actually constructed. Syntax checking is not verification,
and for GUI code that cannot be exercised headlessly the distinction is not
academic.

\section{Middleware Portability: Carolus under ROS2}
\label{sec:ros2}

The supervisor raised a question that reordered the remaining plan: could
Carolus's detection and pose-estimation core be made independent of ROS, so
that the eventual target --- ROS1, ROS2 Humble, or a newer ROS2 release ---
becomes a configuration choice rather than a rewrite?

\subsection{Extraction rather than reimplementation}

Two routes were available: rewrite the pipeline against ROS2 directly, or
extract the existing algorithm into a middleware-free library and wrap it.
Rewriting was judged the more dangerous option, and the reason is specific to
this codebase rather than general. Every hard-won correctness fix in Carolus
shares one failure signature --- the code runs, looks healthy, and publishes
a plausible wrong answer. The hue comparison operating on OpenCV's 0--180
scale rather than 0--360; the orientation conversion of
Section~\ref{sec:quaternion}, wrong twice before it was right; the solver's
convergence diagnostics being computed and then discarded. None of these
announce themselves with a crash. A rewrite has no built-in reason to
rediscover any of them; an extraction carries all of them forward unchanged.

The detection and solver code was therefore separated into
\texttt{carolus\_core}. The claim that it is ROS-free is checked rather than
asserted: the compiled library has an \textbf{empty \texttt{NEEDED} list and
zero unresolved ROS symbols}, verified on x86\_64 and again on the Raspberry
Pi's aarch64. The ROS1 node now links against it instead of containing it.

\subsection{The same source, three toolchains}

A wrapper of roughly 240 lines (\texttt{rclcpp} subscriptions, publishers,
and message conversion --- no algorithm code) was written around that core,
and the identical sources were then compiled under three combinations:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Target} & \textbf{Distribution} & \textbf{Arch.} & \textbf{Build} \\
\midrule
Lab PC            & ROS2 Humble (native)    & x86\_64 & 7.45\,s \\
Lab PC            & ROS2 Jazzy (container)  & x86\_64 & 11.3\,s \\
Raspberry Pi 5    & ROS2 Jazzy (native)     & aarch64 & 46.0\,s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All three build with zero warnings and no source modification. The stronger
result is not that they compile but that they \emph{agree}: run against the
same input, the Humble and Jazzy builds produced the same contour count, the
same solver iteration count and cost, and pose fields matching to eleven
significant digits --- the twelfth being floating-point noise from Ceres
iteration ordering, not a behavioural difference.

\subsection{A silent failure worth recording}

Porting to the Raspberry Pi produced one defect that is worth reporting in
its own right, because it belongs to the same class this project keeps
encountering and would be easy for someone else to lose days to.

Under ROS2 Jazzy on aarch64, \texttt{image\_transport}'s ``raw'' subscriber
plugin \textbf{never delivered a single frame}. The node started normally,
and both \texttt{ros2 node info} and \texttt{ros2 topic info -{}-verbose}
reported the subscription correctly matched to the camera publisher --- yet
the image callback was never invoked. There was no error, no warning, and
nothing in the logs to distinguish this from a camera that simply had
nothing to say. It was confirmed by instrumenting the first line of the
callback itself: zero output across a 30-second run with the camera actively
publishing.

Two hypotheses were tested and rejected before the cause was found. The
board's DDS implementation was cleared by an existing log from an unrelated
node on the same hardware, showing continuous frame reception over 171
seconds under the same middleware. A cable fault was cleared by the kernel
log, which recorded no USB reconnection event. Replacing the call with a
plain \texttt{rclcpp} subscription resolved it immediately and
reproducibly. Since the node had only ever used the ``raw'' transport, the
dependency was removed outright rather than worked around.

\subsection{Detection on both targets; accuracy on neither}

With a physical 4-LED beacon in view, the ROS2 node performs the complete
chain on both machines --- four blobs found, tetrahedron-geometry target
sort, Ceres solve, and a correctly-typed
\texttt{geometry\_msgs/msg/PoseStamped} published on \texttt{/pose}. The two
targets' recorded poses for the same beacon agree to within a few
centimetres.

That agreement should be read carefully, and it is the same distinction this
report draws elsewhere: it establishes that the two ports behave
consistently with \emph{each other}, not that either is numerically correct.
No independent ground truth was involved. \textbf{Pose accuracy was
unverified on both targets}, and there was direct evidence against trusting
it: every solve reported \texttt{converged = 0}, hitting the 30-iteration
ceiling, and the pose was written unconditionally --- so a non-converged
solve returned a finite, plausible value indistinguishable from a good one
unless the diagnostic fields were read. On the lab PC, two visibly different
beacon positions produced translations only a few centimetres apart, which
is not the behaviour of a solver tracking a moved target.
\textbf{Measured directly on the Raspberry Pi 5}: a beacon displaced a real,
ruler-measured 30\,cm moved the reported pose by only 4.28\,mm ---
1.43\,\% of the real move. A miscalibrated camera still responds to a real
displacement at the right order of magnitude; a response this small is not
a calibration error, it is the practical absence of one. This settled, by
measurement rather than inference, that a camera recalibration could not be
expected to fix pose accuracy on its own.

\subsubsection*{Cause found: a coordinate conversion dropped in the port}

Three candidate explanations were open at that point --- the assumed beacon
geometry, the point correspondence, and the solver's fixed initialisation.
The first was eliminated by direct measurement: all six pairwise LED
distances on the beacon in use were checked against the assumed geometry
with a tape measure and matched to within 0.1--0.2\,cm, the instrument's own
resolution. The remaining two were then tested together in a synthetic
harness linking the unmodified solver and correspondence code against a
geometry placed, by bisection, at the exact viewing angle where two of the
beacon's edges subtend equal angles --- the condition a correspondence flip
would depend on.

That test confirmed the correspondence sort is genuinely unstable: at
$\pm1$\,px of noise it selected five different point assignments across 1000
trials and failed outright on 134 of them. \textbf{But it also showed that
effect accounts for centimetres, not metres}, and exposed a larger defect
underneath it. The ROS2 wrapper never converted the sorted image points back
into pixel space before handing them to the solver --- a step the ROS1 node
performs and which was lost in the port, because in the original it sat
inside a loop whose other purpose was populating an Astrobee-specific
message the ROS2 node correctly does not need. The reprojection residual was
therefore comparing a pixel-space prediction against a unit bearing vector:
\textbf{372--640 at the true pose instead of approximately zero}. Minimising
that drives the solution toward cancelling the principal point --- a
quantity shared by all four observations and independent of where the beacon
actually is, which is precisely why the pose barely responded to a real
displacement.

\textbf{Measured before and after on the same node and the same synthetic
input}, with the pre-fix state recovered by rebuilding the previous source
rather than recalled: the solved distance moved from 11.40\,m to 0.679\,m
against a true 0.700\,m, and the solver from \texttt{converged = 0} in 31
iterations to \texttt{converged = 1} in 18. The never-converging solve was
thus a \emph{symptom} of the broken residual rather than an independent
problem --- a residual that cannot approach zero reaches the iteration
ceiling by construction.

One observation from that comparison is worth carrying forward, because it
inverts an intuition: \textbf{the final cost rose} (27.6 to 50.3) while the
pose went from useless to correct. Cost alone is therefore not usable as a
quality gate on this solver.

\textbf{That result was measured, not reasoned --- but only against a
synthetic image, and re-running it on real hardware the same day found a
second real defect before it could give a clean answer.}

\subsubsection*{The real-hardware re-test, and what it found}

A displacement test on a second machine (24.04, a different physical beacon
and camera than the ones above) initially gave a confusing result: 55.3\,\%
of a real 30\,cm move, but with the depth axis moving in the \emph{wrong}
direction --- not the signature of a simple scale error. Checking each
measurement position's own internal stability (repeated stationary samples,
grouped into clusters) explained why: at one distance, 40 stationary samples
formed \textbf{eight distinct pose clusters}, alternating almost frame to
frame, while two other distances on the same rig were each rock-stable ---
the correspondence-sort instability already quantified above, now confirmed
\emph{live} and shown to be geometry-dependent rather than confined to one
narrow synthetic case. Two independently-stable readings, 30\,cm apart,
still disagreed by 135\,mm with the depth axis backwards.

The mechanism is a second defect, distinct from the units mismatch above:
the solver restarts from an identical fixed point on every single frame,
with no memory of the previous solve, so two different starting geometries
are free to converge to two different, disconnected minima --- each
internally stable, neither necessarily correct. Fixed the same day by
warm-starting the solver from its own last converged result rather than a
fixed constant (a backward-compatible, optional parameter; every prior
caller is unaffected). Verified first against a synthetic stationary target
(the solver reached the same answer in 1 iteration instead of 18, byte-for-
byte identical pose), then on hardware: with the fix in place and the node
left running continuously through a real 30\,cm move --- deliberately
\emph{not} restarting the node between readings, since a restart is exactly
what re-exposes the first defect --- the response became \textbf{213.7\,mm
of 300\,mm, 71.2\,\%, with the depth axis moving in the physically correct
direction.}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & pre-fix & units fix alone & units fix + warm start \\
\midrule
Response to a real 300\,mm move & 1.43\,\% & 45--55\,\% (wrong sign) & \textbf{71.2\,\% (correct sign)} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Read precisely: 71.2\,\% is real and correctly signed, not
complete.} The remaining gap is not unexplained --- this camera's own
intrinsics have never been formally calibrated, a question that was
correctly deferred while the pose was not tracking at all and is now, for
the first time, worth asking. The correspondence-sort instability itself
remains open and deliberately unfixed: warm-starting reduces its practical
impact for continuous tracking (a solve seeded from a good neighbour is
less likely to jump to an unrelated minimum than one starting fresh every
frame) but this is inference from the mechanism, not a separate
measurement, and re-acquiring a beacon with no prior pose to warm-start from
remains exposed to it.

\subsection{Maximum update rate}

Every rate observed before this test was bounded by the camera, not by
Carolus: the webcam in use offers only 10 frames per second at its working
resolution. Removing the camera as the limiting factor --- feeding
synthetic frames as fast as a script can produce them, and refusing to
report a result whenever the harness itself might be the bottleneck rather
than the algorithm --- Carolus sustains roughly \textbf{265\,Hz} on the lab
PC and \textbf{52.8\,Hz} on the Raspberry Pi 5, both far faster than any
camera used so far. Running ROS2 Jazzy inside a Docker container rather
than installing it natively cost \textbf{0.6\,\%}, measured on the same
operating system either side of the boundary --- negligible, and confirms
the container used throughout this work is not masking a real performance
cost.

\subsection{What this establishes}

The portability question is answered affirmatively and by measurement: the
same detection and solver sources run under ROS Noetic, ROS2 Humble and ROS2
Jazzy, on two processor architectures, producing matching numerical output.
The middleware is now a deployment choice. What has \emph{not} been shown is
that the resulting pose is accurate --- under any of them.

\section{Results to Date}

The autonomous approach state machine (SEARCH, ALIGN, APPROACH, STOP) is
validated end to end on hardware, including the fan-search coverage pattern
and the LOCATE calibration mode. The F3 EKF chain (beacon detection $\to$
camera-to-beacon transform $\to$ absolute pose $\to$ EKF fusion with wheel
odometry) was validated on hardware with a first in-motion run (1.617\,m
over 37.2\,s): the fused estimate and raw odometry diverged by up to 5.8\,cm
during the run (average 0.6\,cm), with a real but modest signal, consistent
with a short run and a small amount of accumulated drift.

\textbf{This first run should be read as a wiring/sanity check, not as the
drift-reduction demonstration requested by the supervisor}: the course was
too short for meaningful odometric drift to accumulate, and no independent
ground truth was used for comparison --- what the 5.8\,cm measures is
disagreement between two estimates, not the error of either. The dedicated
protocol in Section~\ref{sec:protocol} is designed specifically to produce
that demonstration and has not yet been executed on hardware.

\subsection{The orientation conversion}

Derived, cross-validated, and confirmed on hardware on all three axes with
zero inversions (Section~\ref{sec:quaternion}). This closes the deliverable
the supervisor named as primary.

\subsection{Throughput: 5.95$\times$ from moving one node}
\label{sec:results-throughput}

Carolus was originally run on the lab PC, consuming camera frames streamed
from the Pi. Since the frames are published uncompressed --- roughly
2.76\,MB each --- the transport, not the P4P solve, was the constraint.
Measured like for like on the same beacon the same afternoon, \texttt{/pose}
went from \textbf{2.19\,Hz on the lab PC to 13.04\,Hz on the Pi}. The
bottleneck moved rather than disappeared: on the Pi it is CPU, and the
camera bridge now competes with the solver for it, which is visible in the
camera rate dropping from 30.0\,Hz alone to 16.5\,Hz with Carolus alongside.

\subsection{The attitude estimate is an artefact}

The platform's reported attitude drifts, and for some time this was read as
the chassis physically rotating. It is not. Refitting the reported roll and
pitch against reported \emph{yaw} rather than against time gives
\[
\text{roll} = 21.62\sin(\text{yaw}) + 7.55\cos(\text{yaw}) - 9.47,
\quad R^2 = 0.985,
\]
with pitch following the same form --- the signature of the whole estimate
rotating rigidly about a single axis roughly $11^\circ$ off the body
vertical. That is one constant misaligned gyro-bias vector integrated with
\emph{no gravity correction operating at all}. A flat-floor ground robot
cannot physically roll through $\pm22^\circ$, which is the tell.

It was closed against an independent, calibrated channel rather than against
another estimate. A commanded $14.6^\circ$ gimbal move shifted the image by
182.9\,px, giving 12.5\,px per degree; a genuine $18.1^\circ$ chassis
rotation would therefore move the scene 227\,px. Measured while the attitude
estimate claimed exactly that rotation: \textbf{0.4\,px}, some 567 times
smaller. The consequence is uncomfortable and worth stating --- every drift
figure this project measured on that channel, including some it had already
recorded as findings, inherits the artefact.

\subsection{MINS, characterised rather than assumed}

MINS builds and runs on both machines. Two findings shaped the architecture
more than the build did. Its live-subscribe entry point is \textbf{ROS 2
only}: under Noetic it compiles, initialises, subscribes to nothing and
returns, which is why replay is the supported path here rather than a
fallback. And on the Pi it runs at \textbf{0.7$\times$ real time} against
our own recorded data (camera 118\,ms, IMU 3\,ms per 88\,ms of data) --- and
that figure is optimistic, since the recording itself throttled the sources
below their live rates.

\section{Selected Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:lessons}

A small number of structurally significant issues are worth recording
explicitly, since they were not obvious from surface-level testing and cost
real debugging time:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simultaneous SDK connections silently block motion
  commands.} Two ROS nodes opening independent connections to the robot's
  SDK at the same time causes chassis/gimbal motion commands to stop being
  accepted after a few calls, with no error surfaced -- this was the root
  cause that had blocked autonomous motion in the previous iteration of the
  project and is now avoided by design (a single SDK connection, shared
  across the relevant nodes).
  \item \textbf{A cleared-too-early pose buffer silently broke ALIGN.} The
  pose buffer used to check whether a fresh beacon detection was available
  was being invalidated immediately before the state that needed to read it,
  so ALIGN could never observe a valid pose and would fall back to SEARCH
  before issuing any rotation command. The bug was structural (present since
  the state was first implemented) rather than a hardware or tuning problem.
  \item \textbf{The build environment is not on an officially supported OS
  combination.} ROS Noetic and several of its dependencies target Ubuntu
  20.04; this project's lab PC runs 22.04, and no superuser access is
  available in this environment. Both issues were worked around locally
  (custom \texttt{CMake} config shims, user-space builds) rather than by
  changing the underlying OS or gaining elevated access, which constrains
  how some future dependencies (including MINS) can be installed.
  \item \textbf{Carolus's $\sim$2.5\,Hz publish rate was a network
  bottleneck, and this has since been measured rather than argued.} The
  hypothesis was that transporting uncompressed frames, not the P4P solve,
  was the constraint. Relocating the node confirmed it: 2.19\,Hz to
  13.04\,Hz, like for like. Recorded here as a case where a diagnosis stated
  honestly as a hypothesis was later promoted by measurement rather than by
  repetition.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The recurring failure mode: checks that cannot fail}

The single most expensive pattern on this project was not any individual
bug. It was a class of them: \textbf{an instrument reporting success because
it was structurally incapable of reporting failure}. It recurred often
enough, in enough disguises, that recognising the shape became the most
productive diagnostic habit available.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The P4P solver computes convergence status, final cost and
  iteration count on every frame --- and discards all three. The only
  surviving guard is a finiteness test, which a non-converged solution
  passes trivially. The method that does this is named
  \texttt{computeAndValidatePosesWithRefinement}.

  \item Eight SDK telemetry subscriptions logged ``OK'' on the sole evidence
  that the call had not raised. The boolean each one returns was discarded
  inside a bare \texttt{try}. A subscription the robot refuses returns
  \texttt{False}, quietly.

  \item A credential scanner reported the repository clean for months. Its
  pattern was correct; \texttt{grep} reads text, and the documents holding
  the credentials were PDF and Office containers. \textbf{A check that
  cannot read its input does not report \emph{unknown}; it reports
  \emph{clean}.}

  \item A tool-availability check of the form
  \texttt{cmd --version | head -1 \&\& echo OK} reported four linters
  present. None was installed --- \texttt{head} succeeds on empty input.

  \item A camera-relay process was dead on arrival for five days while the
  GUI logged every command it sent as delivered: standard error discarded,
  success logged immediately after the subprocess was spawned rather than
  after it initialised, and the send path guarded by a bare
  \texttt{except: pass}.

  \item A drift fix was validated at ``1000$\times$ improvement'' against a
  quantity that is \emph{constant by construction} in the very mode being
  tested. The correct reading is that the fix had blinded the instrument
  rather than stilled the robot.

  \item An optimisation skipped republishing a coordinate transform when it
  had not changed. Transforms expire, so on a stationary rig the frame
  stopped existing --- and the failure reported itself as
  ``frame does not exist'', which reads as a missing publisher rather than
  an over-eager one.
\end{itemize}

The counter-practice that emerged is narrow and mechanical: \textbf{prefer a
check with a demonstrated way to come back negative}. In practice this meant
validating detectors against planted defects before trusting them on real
data; deliberately sabotaging a documentation checker to confirm it would
notice; proving a refactor null by comparing 35 composed transforms
element-wise; and, when a positive control was needed, checking that the
control itself had worked --- one such test ``proved'' a camera blind while
publishing to the wrong message type, and the tell was a suspiciously high
correlation confidence on a genuinely static scene.

\subsection{Measuring things that move while you measure them}

A second cluster of lessons concerns instruments that work but do not
measure what they appear to.

Several early figures were repeatability dressed as accuracy: a pose stable
to sub-millimetre across frames on a static scene says nothing about its
distance from the truth, and a controller stopping 3\,mm from its own
setpoint measures the controller, not the position. Both were recorded at
the time as if they were accuracy results.

The correction that generalised best was to insist the reference be
independent of the thing under test. The attitude artefact
(Section~\ref{sec:quaternion} and Results) was only settled by an optical
channel calibrated by a commanded motion of known size --- not by comparing
two estimates that shared an origin. Similarly, when three measurement
channels disagreed, the resolution was to check the outlier's own health
rather than take the majority: the camera channel was returning hundreds of
pixels of motion because the room lights were off and the correlator was
matching noise against noise. Two trustworthy channels beat three when the
third is measuring nothing.
